### A baby-step, one-by-one walkthrough of every image and video slot on the site

I went through your codebase and counted **138 unique placeholder photo slots** and **2 video slots**, currently all filled with random `picsum.photos` images. Below, I'll teach you what each category needs, why it matters for the "ultra world-class law firm" feeling, and exactly what to ask a photographer (or search for in stock libraries) for each one.

## PART 1: The Golden Rules (read this before anything else)

Think of every photo on this site as one frame from the same movie. Right now, the placeholders are random strangers, random buildings — nothing matches. A world-class firm's site feels expensive because every image was shot (or chosen) by the same eye, under the same rules. Here are those rules, explained simply:

### Rule 1: One consistent color temperature
Your brand colors are navy (`#0E1B2B`), gold (`#B8873B`), and warm paper white (`#F8F5EF`). Every photo should be graded (color-corrected) toward **cool, deep shadows and warm, golden highlights** — think of the lighting in a nice hotel lobby at dusk, or a bank's boardroom with one warm lamp on. Avoid:
- Flat, bright, "corporate stock photo" lighting (the kind with everyone smiling too hard under fluorescent office lights)
- Blue-white or greenish color casts
- Oversaturated, cartoonish colors

**In practice:** if you hire a photographer, show them the hex codes above and say "grade everything toward this palette." If you're buying stock photos, filter for "moody," "editorial," or "cinematic" style, not "bright and cheerful."

### Rule 2: Shallow depth of field
Notice how in expensive law firm photography, the background is often a soft blur while the subject (a person, a document, a skyline) is sharp? That's a wide-aperture lens (f/1.8–f/2.8) or a long lens compressing the background. It's what separates "iPhone snapshot" from "hired a real photographer." Ask for this specifically.

### Rule 3: No stock-photo clichés
Skip: people shaking hands over a desk, a gavel, scales of justice, a magnifying glass on a document, a diverse group pointing at a laptop while laughing. These scream "generic stock photo" and undercut the premium feeling instantly. Instead: candid-feeling working moments, quiet confident portraits, real architecture, real cities.

### Rule 4: Wide negative space
Almost every hero/banner image on this site has text placed over the top or bottom third. That means the photo needs **empty, uncluttered space** in that zone — a plain wall, open sky, a blurred-out background — so the text stays readable. A photo that's "busy" edge-to-edge will fight with your headlines. I'll flag exactly where the text sits for each hero.

### Rule 5: Real people need real releases
If you use actual photos of your actual attorneys and offices, that's the single biggest thing that will make this site feel real instead of "template." If you use stock photos of models standing in for attorneys, that's fine for a placeholder/demo but you'd never publish it as a real firm — a client Googling "Jane Smith attorney" and finding a stock model's face elsewhere online is a real reputational problem. Flag this clearly to whoever handles your real launch.

## PART 2: Category by category

### Category A — The Homepage Hero Slideshow (4 photos, the single most important images on the site)
**Where:** the very first thing anyone sees, full-bleed background, crossfading every few seconds, with "Global counsel. Local instinct." typed over it.
**Specs:** 1800×1200px minimum (landscape), JPG, ideally shot at 3600×2400 and compressed down for crispness on retina phone screens.
**What to get, one at a time:**
1. **Attorneys in conference** — a real (or real-feeling) shot of 3–5 people mid-conversation around a conference table, shot from a slight angle (not straight-on), soft window light, nobody looking at the camera. Feels observed, not posed.
2. **International skyline** — a global city skyline at dusk/blue hour (that in-between time when the sky is deep blue and building lights are on). Chicago, NYC, London, Singapore, or Dubai all work. This is the "we're a global firm" beat.
3. **Boardroom negotiation** — tighter shot, maybe 2–3 people, hands and documents visible, dramatic side lighting. This is the "high-stakes deal" beat.
4. **City skyline at dusk** — a second, different skyline for variety as the slideshow loops.

**Baby step tip:** all four need empty space in the *bottom-left two-thirds* of the frame, since that's where the headline and buttons sit. A photo where the important visual subject is in the top-right or far background works best here.

### Category B — Interior Page Hero Banners (about 20 photos)
**Where:** every interior page (About, Practice Areas, Industries, Attorneys, Locations, Matters, Testimonials, Blog, Press, Publications, Events, Glossary, FAQ, Quiz→Consultation, Checklist, Careers, Diversity, Sustainability, Pro Bono, Alumni, Recognition, Glance) has one banner image at the very top, behind a dark gradient overlay with the page title centered over it.
**Specs:** 1800×900 or 1800×1000px, landscape, JPG.
**What to get:** because there's already a dark gradient overlay built into the code (so text stays readable no matter what), these can be *moodier and more atmospheric* than the homepage hero — you have more freedom here. Good options per page:
- **About** — the exterior or lobby of your actual flagship office building
- **Attorneys** — a candid shot of several attorneys walking through an office hallway, motion-blurred slightly
- **Practice Areas / Industries** — an abstract architectural shot (glass, steel, dramatic angles) rather than people, since this page is about categories, not a specific moment
- **Locations** — an airport, a world map lit up, or a skyline collage
- **Testimonials** — a warm, softly-lit handshake or conversation moment (one of the only places a "meeting" photo is appropriate, because the content itself is about client relationships)
- **Careers / Diversity / Pro Bono / Sustainability** — genuine culture photos: people at a whiteboard, a team lunch, a volunteer event — these should feel the *least* corporate and the *most* human of anything on the site
- **Press / Publications / Blog / Events / FAQ / Glossary** — can reuse a strong architectural or skyline shot; these pages are read for content, not mood, so don't over-invest here

**Baby step tip:** because the title text is centered (not left-aligned like the homepage), these photos should have their visual interest spread evenly, or concentrated in the corners, with a calmer middle.

### Category C — Attorney Headshots (14 people, 2 photos each = 28 files)
**Where:** every attorney bio page, the attorney directory grid, "related attorneys" widgets sitewide, and vCard downloads.
**Specs:** each attorney needs two crops:
- **500×667px (3:4 portrait)** — the main "hero" shot on their own bio page
- **400×540px (also 3:4, slightly different crop)** — used in directory cards and sidebar widgets

**This is the category to prioritize highest** if you're doing any real photography, because attorney trust is the entire point of a law firm's website. Here's exactly what to brief a photographer for:
- **Same backdrop for everyone.** Either a plain seamless studio backdrop in a warm neutral tone, or the same real office corner/window for every single attorney. Never mix studio shots with candid office shots — that inconsistency reads as "we couldn't get everyone in the same room," which undercuts trust.
- **Same lighting setup for everyone.** A softbox key light at 45°, slight rim light from behind. Once you find a setup that looks good on one person, use the exact same one for all 16 — don't let it drift session to session.
- **Same crop and eye-line.** Head and shoulders, eyes roughly one-third from the top of the frame, subject looking directly at camera (approachable) or slightly off-camera (more editorial/aspirational) — pick one style and use it for everyone.
- **Wardrobe guidance:** solid, dark, or neutral colors (navy, charcoal, black, white); no busy patterns, no logos, nothing that clashes with the gold accent color.
- **Expression:** a small, confident, closed or slightly-open smile — not a big grin, not stone-faced. Think "I trust this person to handle a $300M deal," not "I trust this person to host a birthday party."

**Baby step tip:** if a real photo shoot with all 16 attorneys isn't possible right now, the single best stand-in is **consistent, same-session AI-generated portraits** at high resolution, styled identically — that at least gives you visual consistency across the team, which matters more than any individual photo being perfect. Just don't publish AI-generated "attorneys" as a real, live firm — that's a trust and legal-ethics problem, not just a design one.

### Category D — Global Office Interiors (14 offices)
**Where:** the Locations page, one photo per office (Chicago, New York, DC, Dubai, Frankfurt, Hong Kong, Johannesburg, LA, London, Paris, Singapore, São Paulo, Sydney, Tokyo).
**Specs:** 500×380px, landscape, JPG.
**What to get:** either (a) a real interior shot of each actual office's reception or conference room, or (b) if you don't have real offices yet, a recognizable **skyline or landmark shot for that city** works as an honest placeholder (a real firm should eventually swap these for real interiors, but a skyline is a defensible "here's roughly where we are" image, unlike a fake photo pretending to be your literal office). Keep the same warm color grade from Rule 1 across all 14 so the page feels like one coherent set, not 14 different photographers' work.

### Category E — Practice Area Images (about 20 photos)
**Where:** the Practice Areas page, one photo per practice (Corporate & M&A, Litigation & Arbitration, Private Equity, Restructuring, Family Law, Estate Planning, Antitrust, Banking, Capital Markets, Criminal Defense/White Collar, Employment, Energy & Project Finance, ESG, Immigration, Insurance, IP, Data Privacy & Cybersecurity, Real Estate, Sports & Entertainment, Tax).
**Specs:** mostly 700×500px landscape, a few 400×560px portrait.
**What to get:** this is the category most tempting to fill with clichés (gavels, scales) — resist it. Instead, go abstract and industry-adjacent:
- **Corporate/M&A, Private Equity, Capital Markets** → trading floors, skyscraper facades, handshake-free "closing a deal" energy (documents being signed, champagne *not* included)
- **Litigation, Criminal Defense, Antitrust** → courthouse architecture, empty courtroom benches, dramatic single-figure-in-hallway shots
- **Family Law, Estate Planning** → warm, human, home-adjacent imagery (hands, a kitchen table, generations) — the one place in this whole category where "human warmth" over "corporate polish" is correct
- **Energy, Real Estate, Immigration, Tech/IP** → the physical subject matter itself: solar/wind infrastructure, architecture, a passport and boarding pass, a server room or circuit board close-up

### Category F — Industry Group Images (10 industries, 2 crops each = 20 photos)
**Where:** the Industries page. Financial Services, Energy & Infrastructure, Healthcare & Life Sciences, Technology, Consumer & Retail, Real Estate, Transportation, Media & Telecom, Industrials & Manufacturing, Private Capital.
**Specs:** a 500×620px portrait crop and a 700×500px landscape crop for each.
**What to get:** same logic as Category E — go straight to the physical subject matter (a hospital corridor for Healthcare, a shipping port for Transportation, a data center for Tech) rather than generic "business people in an office" photos. This category benefits the most from stock photography, since it's less about your firm specifically and more about signaling deep sector knowledge.

### Category G — Case Studies, Blog & Press Imagery (about 25 photos)
**Where:** homepage case-study cards, the full Blog hub and its 3 articles, Press & Media, Publications, Events.
**Specs:** varies — blog thumbnails are 500×340px, featured blog images are 1400×700px, in-article images are 900×500px.
**What to get:** editorial-style photography — think "photo that would run next to a Wall Street Journal article," not "stock photo illustrating the concept of law." For the 3 existing blog articles (Corporate Compliance, Family Law, Estate Planning) match the tone from Category E above.

### Category H — Testimonials & Client Trust (about 10 photos)
**Where:** Testimonials page, homepage social proof section.
**Specs:** small circular avatars at 80×80px, plus one 1600×700px backdrop image.
**What to get:** if these are real client testimonials, always get real client photos with signed permission — a testimonial with a fake photo is a legal and ethical problem if discovered. If testimonials are illustrative placeholders (as your README currently notes), keep avatars generic and low-detail rather than photorealistic headshots of "fake clients," to avoid the same issue as the fake-attorney problem above.

### Category I — Video Content (2 slots — this is the category most people forget)
**Where:** two spots reference actual video, not just static images:
1. **`careersvideo1`, alt "Life at the firm"** — a video poster/thumbnail on the Careers page, 1600×700px, meant to sit behind a play button for a culture video.
2. **`videoposter1`, alt "Firm overview film"** — homepage "Watch Our Story" button opens a video modal; this is the poster frame shown before playing.

**What you actually need here, in baby steps:**
- **The firm overview film** (60–90 seconds): a short cinematic piece — sweeping shots of your office(s), a few attorneys talking briefly to camera about why they do this work, a voiceover or on-screen text with your tagline. This is the single highest-leverage video on the whole site, since it plays right after your strongest headline.
- **The culture/careers video** (60–90 seconds): more casual, documentary-style — a day in the life, attorneys and staff talking about growth and culture, shot handheld or on a gimbal rather than locked-off tripod shots, to feel more human than the polished firm overview film.
- **Technical specs for both:** 1080p minimum, ideally 4K source footage graded down, MP4 (H.264), with a captioned/subtitled version too since many visitors will watch muted.
- **The poster frame** for each video (the still image shown before someone presses play) should be the single best-looking frame from the video itself — not a separate photo — so there's no jarring mismatch when playback starts.

## PART 4: How to actually source these, in order of budget

1. **Best (real firm launch):** hire one photographer for one or two full days — shoot all 16 attorney headshots in one consistent session, plus the office interiors, plus B-roll for the video, all in the same visit. This is dramatically cheaper and more consistent than piecing it together over time.
2. **Good (budget-conscious):** license editorial-style stock from a paid library (Getty, Stocksy, Offset) rather than free libraries — the free ones are exactly where "generic stock photo" clichés come from, and a trained eye can tell instantly.
3. **Fine for a demo/portfolio (not a real launch):** consistent, same-session AI-generated imagery, styled to the Golden Rules above — as long as it's clearly a demo and not presented as real attorneys to real prospective clients.
